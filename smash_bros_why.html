How It Works

The four tiers are a thinking ladder, not a rank ladder

Every question is tagged to a tier. The tier is not “how good you are at Smash” — it is what kind of cognitive work the question demands of you. A locals-winning player who has never interrogated a habit can be stuck at Tier 1 thinking, and a mid-ladder online player who reasons carefully can operate at Tier 2.

The four tiers of analytical demand
TierDemandThe question sounds like
1 — IdentifyName what happened, and separate what you observed from what you concluded.“What actually took that stock, as opposed to what I felt took it?”
2 — ExplainTrace the cause through the systems of the game: space, percent, option coverage, frames.“What made that stock unwinnable ten seconds before I died?”
3 — EvaluateJudge a decision on the information available at the time, not on how it turned out.“Given what I knew then, was there a better option — and could I have executed it?”
4 — ModelReason about your own reasoning, and about an opponent reasoning about you.“What is my model of this player, how would I know if it is wrong, and what do they think I am about to do?”

How the engine grows with you

  • Each of the eight tracks carries its own depth value from 1.0 to 4.0. Answering well pushes that track up; missing pulls it down. Depth falls faster than it climbs, which keeps you near the edge of what you can actually do rather than comfortably inside it.
  • The engine serves your weakest unmastered track most often, so your time goes where the return is highest — with a deliberate minority of questions from stronger tracks so they do not decay.
  • Some questions are archetype-specific. Where the failure mode genuinely differs by character type — approaching versus being approached, for instance — the engine prefers the version that matches what you main. Most of the material is deliberately archetype-neutral, because the reasoning transfers between characters even when the specifics do not.
  • Mastery is capped by evidence. Proficient requires clearing a Tier 3 item. Mastered requires Tier 4. Grinding Tier 1 plateaus you at Familiar, which is the honest result.
  • Items you get wrong are returned to you later, not immediately — spaced by several questions, because re-answering something you just saw tests your short-term memory rather than your understanding.
  • Hints are a ladder: the first reframes the question, the second narrows the field, the third nearly answers it. Using hints reduces credit, because a hinted answer is weaker evidence that you can do it unaided.

Why the content avoids specific frame data

You will notice this module almost never quotes a frame number, a kill percent or a character matchup. That is deliberate. There are dozens of characters and thousands of interactions between them, so any table printed here would be both incomplete and beside the point — and looking your own numbers up is the skill. A player who knows their opponent's fastest out-of-shield option because they measured it in training mode is doing analysis. A player who memorized a tier list is doing trivia. The reasoning is what transfers between characters, between matchups, and between games in the series; the numbers are homework you do against your own eight or ten worst matchups.

What this module cannot do

Nothing here grades your written answers — there is no model behind this page. Free-response items are scored by an explicit rubric that you apply to your own answer after reading an expert response. That is a real and well-evidenced technique (self-explanation against a worked example), and it works, but it runs on your honesty. Every item also has a Copy coaching prompt button that packages your answer, the scenario and your current depth into a prompt for an AI assistant that can read and respond to it. The AI Coach tab builds a fuller version of that.
