Review Protocols

A protocol is a repeatable procedure you run on real footage. Review without a procedure collapses into re-watching yourself play, which feels like work and teaches almost nothing. These unlock by depth, because each one assumes the ones below it are already habits.

Read this before you start Apex has no replay viewer. Unlike most games this module's siblings cover, you cannot go back and watch a match you did not record — so the single highest-leverage setup step is turning on rolling instant replay (Windows Game Bar, GeForce or AMD instant replay, or OBS replay buffer) and binding the save key. You almost never know a moment was worth studying until after it has happened; a thirty-second buffer means you can save it anyway. Every protocol below assumes you have footage, and none of them work on memory.

How It Works

The four tiers are a thinking ladder, not a rank ladder

Every question is tagged to a tier. The tier is not “how good you are at Apex” — it is what kind of cognitive work the question demands of you. A Master who has never interrogated a habit can be stuck at Tier 1 thinking, and a Gold player who reasons carefully can operate at Tier 2.

The four tiers of analytical demand
TierDemandThe question sounds like
1 — IdentifyName what happened, and separate what you observed from what you concluded.“What actually killed me, as opposed to what I felt killed me?”
2 — ExplainTrace the cause through the systems of the game: ring, terrain, resources, sound, time.“What made that fight unwinnable a minute before it started?”
3 — EvaluateJudge a decision on the information available at the time, not on how it turned out.“Given what I could hear and see then, was there a better option — and could I have executed it?”
4 — ModelReason about your own reasoning, and about eighteen other squads reasoning about you.“What is my model of this lobby, how would I know if it is wrong, and what do they think I am about to do?”

How the engine grows with you

  • Each of the nine tracks carries its own depth value from 1.0 to 4.0. Answering well pushes that track up; missing pulls it down. Depth falls faster than it climbs, which keeps you near the edge of what you can actually do rather than comfortably inside it.
  • The engine serves your weakest unmastered track most often, so your time goes where the return is highest — with a deliberate minority of questions from stronger tracks so they do not decay.
  • Questions are weighted toward your job in the squad. A shotcaller and an entry fragger get different versions of the same lesson, because the information each of them has, and the failures each of them owns, are different.
  • Mastery is capped by evidence. Proficient requires clearing a Tier 3 item. Mastered requires Tier 4. Grinding Tier 1 plateaus you at Familiar, which is the honest result.
  • Items you get wrong are returned to you later, not immediately — spaced by several questions, because re-answering something you just saw tests your short-term memory rather than your understanding.
  • Hints are a ladder: the first reframes the question, the second narrows the field, the third nearly answers it. Using hints reduces credit, because a hinted answer is weaker evidence that you can do it unaided.

Why a battle royale needs its own analysis discipline

Apex punishes review in a way that arena games do not, for three structural reasons, and every design choice in this module is a response to one of them.

  • Enormous variance. Twenty squads, one life, a randomized ring and randomized loot. A good decision loses often enough that outcomes are a terrible teacher. This is why the Decision Quality track exists, and why several questions are about how much evidence a conclusion actually needs.
  • Severe survivorship bias in your own footage. You review deaths, because deaths are what hurt. But every death ends with an aggressive act, so a method that only examines deaths will always conclude you were too aggressive — including in the weeks when you were too passive. Reviewing the fights you won is not optional here; it is the control group.
  • No replay system. You cannot retrieve a match you did not record, and you cannot see what the enemy saw. So the analysis has to lean on capture you set up in advance, on audio reconstruction, and on inference from evidence — deathboxes, squad counters, ring flares — rather than on omniscient camera work.

Why the content avoids exact numbers

You will notice this module almost never quotes a cooldown, a damage value, a ring timer or a ranked entry cost. That is deliberate, and it is two things at once. Apex changes substantially every season — legends are reworked, classes gain and lose perks, armour and crafting rules have been rebuilt more than once, and the ranked scoring formula has been redesigned repeatedly. Any number printed here would rot. More importantly, looking the number up yourself is the skill. A player who knows the enemy Legend's escape is down because they counted it is doing analysis. A player who memorized a wiki page is doing trivia. The durable content is the reasoning; the numbers are homework you do against the current patch.

What this module cannot do

Nothing here grades your written answers — there is no model behind this page. Free-response items are scored by an explicit rubric that you apply to your own answer after reading an expert response. That is a real and well-evidenced technique (self-explanation against a worked example), and it works, but it runs on your honesty. Every item also has a Copy coaching prompt button that packages your answer, the scenario and your current depth into a prompt for an AI assistant that can read and respond to it. The AI Coach tab builds a fuller version of that.
